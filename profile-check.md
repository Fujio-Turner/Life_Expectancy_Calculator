# Profile check

Checked in November 2026 against the calculator on this page. Each profile was run through the same model the chart uses, then a sample was entered on the form. Years below are the change in the median age at death versus peers, unless a row says life expectancy. Peers are the CDC table for that age, sex, and origin. The page rounds those years to a whole number.

The hazards were left as they are. One display fix was made: body mass index is now rounded to one decimal before it is scored, which is the same number the year row prints.

The same BMI removes more years for men than for women. At age 40, BMI 40 is about 2 years for a woman and about 6 years for a man. That split is the Lancet 2016 North America slope, made a bit steeper for men and a bit flatter for women. Above BMI 55 the years stop falling. BMI 60 matches BMI 55. The raw hazard is capped at 4.5, and the BMI itself is capped at 55.

The curve is flat from BMI 20 through 25, then rises smoothly. It is not flat across 18.5. At age 40 the row jumps from about −1 year just under 18.5 to about +1 year for women, and from about 0 to about +2 for men. A second, smaller step sits at BMI 20. Those cuts are the published underweight groups (hazard 1.51 below 18.5, and 1.13 from 18.5 up to 20). Smoothing them would invent a curve the study did not publish.

Before the fix, 106 lb at 5 ft 3.5 in was a true BMI of 18.48. The page printed BMI 18.5 and scored the underweight side, so the row said −1 year. One pound later it said +1 year. The hazard now uses the printed one-decimal BMI. That 106 lb case prints BMI 18.5 and +1 year. The step a visitor can still see is between a printed 18.4 and a printed 18.5.

Body size is applied from age 35. A 30-year-old still loses years, because the later ages use the hazard. At age 30 the current-year hazard is 1. The median still moves.

## Smoking

Le, Mendez, and Warner, American Journal of Preventive Medicine, 2024, give years of life expectancy lost by people who keep smoking, versus people who never smoked. The figures are for both sexes together: 9.1, 8.3, 7.3, 5.9, and 4.4 years at ages 35, 45, 55, 65, and 75.

This page, current versus never, in years of life expectancy:

| Age | Published, both sexes | Women | Men |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 35 | 9.1 | 8.7 | 9.8 |
| 45 | 8.3 | 8.3 | 9.3 |
| 55 | 7.3 | 7.6 | 8.3 |
| 65 | 5.9 | 6.1 | 6.5 |
| 75 | 4.4 | 4.2 | 4.2 |

Women are within half a year. Men are within about a year, on the high side in middle age, because the same hazard sits on a higher male death rate. Averaging the sexes stays inside a year of the published figure. The knots stay.

The year row is versus peers, and peers include former and current smokers. That row is smaller than current versus never. At age 55, current versus never is about 8 years of life expectancy for a woman and about 8 years for a man, while the median gap versus peers is about 8 years for a woman and about 10 years for a man. The higher-risk example is a 55-year-old man who smokes now. The form shows Current smoking −8 years, which is the median versus peers (−7.7 years before rounding), not the 10-year gap versus people who never smoked.

Jha and colleagues, New England Journal of Medicine, 2013, estimated survival from 25 to 80 at about 70% versus 38% for women and 61% versus 26% for men, never versus current. From age 25 this page gives 72% versus 43% for women and 60% versus 27% for men. Jha’s “about 11 years for women and 12 for men” is that long survival contrast. It is not the remaining-years figure at age 40, and it is not the year row.

Quit more than 10 years ago is a small remaining gap versus never, about 1 to 2 years of life expectancy at these ages. Quit within 10 years sits between that and current smoking. Those two choices are not Le’s “quit at this exact birthday.”

## Diabetes, income, and the other fields

At age 50, diabetes versus peers is about 4 years earlier for a woman and about 5 years for a man. Versus someone without diabetes it is about 5 years for a woman and about 6 years for a man. The 2011 study’s figure is about 6 years at age 50 versus no diabetes. Men land on that figure. Women land about a year under it, on the same hazard of 1.80. The hazard stays. The methods sentence “about 4 years” and “about 5 years” is the women’s side of this split.

Own earnings at age 40, median versus peers:

| Quarter | Men | Women |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Bottom | −5.4 | −3.1 |
| Second | −0.9 | −0.8 |
| Third | +1.4 | +1.0 |
| Top | +4.7 | +3.4 |

The tooltip’s “about 5 years for men and about 3 years for women” is those outer quarters, rounded, at age 40. The middle quarters are about 1 year. At age 70 the outer quarters are about 2 to 3 years. A 25-year-old is close to the age-40 result, because the hazard starts at 40 and the years before that stay on the CDC table. Marital status is left off the form.

For a 40-year-old woman, no leisure-time activity is about 2 years earlier, some activity is about 0, and meeting the guideline is about 1 year later. Some activity is hazard 0.80 against a U.S. mix that is already near that, so the row is flat on purpose.

A past heart attack at age 55 is about 6 years earlier. A past stroke is about 7 years. Another serious illness does not move the ages.

Diagnosed COPD at age 55, with smoking left blank, is about 3 years earlier for a woman. The same COPD plus “never smoked” is about 1 year earlier, because the never-smoker credit offsets most of the COPD hazard. On oxygen, with smoking left blank, is about 5 years earlier. Kidney disease stage 3 at age 55 is about 4 years earlier. Kidney failure or dialysis is about 16 years earlier. An explicit “no” on COPD or kidney disease is a fraction of a year later. An empty box is not that credit.

## Stacks, and the two caps

The higher-risk example, a man of 55 who smokes now, weighs 240 lb, and has diabetes, has a median of 67.5 against peers at 82.4. The form shows Around 68–69, Current smoking −8, BMI 35.4 −2, Diabetes −5, and Total −15. Added alone, those three rows are −14.7 years. Together they are −14.9. Close, on this stack.

Smoking, BMI 35, and the bottom income quarter at age 40 for a man are −9.0, −2.3, and −5.4 alone, which add to −16.6. Together they are −18.4. The rows do not add. Income is outside the small overlap reduction used for illnesses, and the survival curve is not a straight line.

Two stacks hit the hazard cap of 6 and land on the same ages. For a man of 55, kidney failure plus current smoking, and kidney failure plus the bottom income quarter, both have a median of 65.2. Failure alone is already a hazard near 5.7, so the cap leaves no room for the second fact. The cap stays. Raising it would treat a product the studies did not measure as if it were measured.

The floor is 0.35. A man of 40 who never smoked, meets the activity guideline, has BMI 22.5, has no COPD, has no kidney disease, and is in the top earnings quarter would otherwise have a hazard of about 0.18 at that age. The page holds him at 0.35 and still gives him about 10 years later than peers. Those credits describe overlapping groups. High earnings already include less smoking and a different body size. The floor is the guard against multiplying them.

## What to leave, and what could be shown later

Leave the smoking knots, the BMI curve, the diabetes hazard, the income quarters, and both caps. Each one lands on the published comparison it was built for, or on a sex difference that the same hazard produces. The odd steps are the published BMI groups at 18.5 and 20, the age gates, the cap of 6, and the floor of 0.35.

A short note on the page could say four things, if that content is added later:

- The year row is versus peers. Current smoking versus people who never smoked is larger.
- The U.S. average weight is already near the peers, so the BMI row is smaller than a comparison with BMI 22.5.
- At age 40, the outer income quarters are about 5 years for men and about 3 years for women. The middle quarters are about 1 year.
- Below a printed BMI of 18.5 the row steps down, because that is the published underweight group. One pound can cross it.
