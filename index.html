Stats breakdown

  • The big number runs from your median age at death to the average, rounded to the nearest year. Around 68–69 means those two ages.
  • Amber is the U.S. all-people range: men’s median age at death to women’s. It moves with age, and it stays put when sex, origin, body size, health, or personal income change. Teal is peers of your age, sex, and origin on the CDC table. Height, weight, activity, smoking, illness, and personal income are not in that band. Blue is you, including those. The red dashed line is your current age.
  • The line’s height is how many of 100 people in your group die at that age. It is a share of the life table, not a census count.
  • Blank fields leave the CDC table. A filled field shows your years added (+) or removed (−) from your peers’ median, in the table under the chart.
  • For education and entertainment. A population model, not a forecast for you and not medical advice. Nothing you type leaves this browser. The marks at the top name the public data. They are not an endorsement.

The base is the complete period life table for 2023 from the National Center for Health Statistics. A period table applies this year’s death rates at every age to a hypothetical group. It does not guess future medical gains. NCHS built the rates from final 2023 deaths, Medicare records at the oldest ages, and July 1, 2023 population estimates from the U.S. Census Bureau’s blended base. The Census Bureau supplies the population denominator. It does not publish this life expectancy itself.

Life expectancy is an average age at death, counting years left from the birthday you entered. The median is the age half of that life table lives past. The headline rounds those two ages to the nearest year. Around 68–69 means a median of 68 and an average of 69. A tenth of a year would look more exact than this estimate is.

With no body size, smoking, or condition filled in, the result is the CDC table. Checked health details multiply the chance of dying in each future year, using 1 − (1 − q)h, where q is the CDC probability for that year of age and h is a hazard ratio from the studies below. The ratio is scaled so a person who matches today’s U.S. mix of smokers, activity, and body sizes stays on the CDC table.

Age, sex, origin and race NCHS life tables, 2023 Selects one of 12 sex-by-group tables. “Another group” uses the U.S. total, because NCHS does not publish a separate complete table for it.
Height and weight Global BMI Mortality Collaboration, Lancet, 2016. CDC adult BMI categories. NHANES measured percentiles, August 2021–August 2023. NCHS measured mean height and weight, adults 20 and older, 2015–2018. BMI from 20 to 25 is the reference. Above 25, risk rises by 1.29 times per 5 BMI points, the North America slope, a bit steeper for men than women, and weaker after midlife. Applied from age 35. The U.S. average uses the published NHANES percentiles from the 5th through the 95th. The 1st and 99th points, men 18.5 and 50 and women 17.5 and 52, only close the tails. They are not published NHANES percentiles. Choosing sex fills the measured means: men 69.0 inches and 199.8 pounds, women 63.5 inches and 170.8 pounds. Clear the fields to leave body size out.
Smoking CDC: smokers lose at least 10 years versus people who never smoked. Le and colleagues, American Journal of Preventive Medicine, 2024, for the age pattern. Current, long-quit, and recent-quit hazards are set so the gap versus never-smokers lands within about a year of those published gaps from ages 35 to 75. The mix used here is about 67% never, 22% former, and 11% current. Only current smoking is the published NHIS figure, 11.0% in 2023.
Diabetes Emerging Risk Factors Collaboration, New England Journal of Medicine, 2011. Hazard ratio 1.80 versus no diabetes, already adjusted for smoking and BMI. About 6 years of life lost at age 50 in that study. Applied from age 30, then divided by the U.S. prevalence factor (12% of the population has diabetes, diagnosed or undiagnosed) so the CDC average is unchanged. On the 2023 U.S. total table, at age 50, the median is about 4 years earlier for women and about 5 years earlier for men than others the same age and sex, and about 5 years earlier for women and about 6 years earlier for men than someone without diabetes. The 2011 study’s figure was about 6 years versus someone without diabetes. A diagnosis in early adulthood can take more years than this average.
Heart attack or stroke Emerging Risk Factors Collaboration, JAMA, 2015. About 2.0 after a heart attack and 2.1 after a stroke, versus people with neither, adjusted for age and sex. Applied from age 30 on top of the U.S. average rate. That is slightly cautious, because the average already includes some people with those histories. Two conditions together are pulled a little under a full product, matching that paper.
Leisure-time activity Arem and colleagues, JAMA Internal Medicine, 2015. Versus no leisure-time activity: some activity under 7.5 MET-hours a week, hazard 0.80; meeting the guideline at 1 to 2 times the minimum, hazard 0.69. CDC, NHIS 2024: 26.2% do no leisure-time activity and 47.2% meet the aerobic guideline. The rest, 26.6%, is the remainder. Applied from age 18. The three hazards are divided by that U.S. mix, so an average adult stays on the CDC table. Everyone who meets the guideline uses 0.69. Extra activity past the guideline is not a separate choice. A physical job is not counted. Not specified keeps the mix. Activity is scaled like smoking, outside the overlap reduction used for illnesses.
COPD Zhao and colleagues, International Journal of COPD, 2025, 27 cohorts. Within the same smoking status, diagnosed COPD is 1.41 for people who never smoked, 1.37 for former smokers, and 1.48 for current smokers. Severe COPD is 2.18. The unadjusted overall 1.80 is not used. NCHS, NHIS 2023: 3.8% of adults have diagnosed COPD. Women 4.1%, men 3.4%. Applied from age 40. The diagnosed hazard is divided by a factor that uses 3.8% and a base hazard of 1.45, so the U.S. average stays on the CDC table. If smoking is not specified, diagnosed COPD uses 1.45. On oxygen or hospitalized uses 2.18. When the person also smokes now or quit within 10 years, that severe hazard is reduced the same way as a heart attack or stroke. Oxygen has no separate share of the population in this correction. Never diagnosed is a small credit. Not specified is not a credit.
Kidney disease Writing Group for the CKD Prognosis Consortium, JAMA, 2023. Filter rate 45 to 59 versus 90 to 104, with some albumin in the urine (10 to 29), all-cause hazard 1.6. With almost no albumin, that hazard is 1.3. Go and colleagues, New England Journal of Medicine, 2004. Filter rate under 15 versus 60 or higher, all-cause hazard 5.9. NHANES 2017–March 2020: stage 3 is 5.1% of adults, stage 4 is 0.3%, and stage 5 is 0.2%. Applied from age 40. Stage 3, not on dialysis, uses 1.6. That is the stage 3a figure with some albumin, so later stage 3 and stage 4 are understated. Stage 4 is not its own choice. Kidney failure or dialysis uses 5.9, the closest published bucket, and the prevalence correction uses the 0.2% in stage 5. The hazards are divided by the U.S. mix of those two groups. COPD and kidney disease join the same small overlap reduction used when two or more illnesses are on. Never having it is a small credit. Not specified is not a credit. The CDC 14% figure is stages 1 through 4 and is shown only as a comparison, not as this hazard.
Personal income Chetty, Stepner, Abraham, and colleagues, JAMA, 2016. Individual earnings in tax records, ranked within sex and age, ages 40 to 76, deaths in 2001–2014. Online table 16. Not household income. The household gap in that paper, 14.6 years for men and 10.1 for women between the richest 1% and the poorest 1%, is not the figure used here. Four quarters of people your sex and age. The hazard is that quarter’s death rate divided by the rate for everyone of that sex and age, pooled across those years. It changes with age and is held at the age-76 value after that. Before age 40 it is not applied. At age 40, income can raise or lower life expectancy by about 5 years for men and about 3 years for women. Dollar hints are the quarter’s average own earnings in 2012 dollars, restated with the CPI-U annual average (2012 = 229.594, 2024 = 313.689). Blank leaves the mix. The association already includes smoking, body size, and origin, so the row overlaps those.

Your analysis, the first tab under the chart, is written from the fields you fill. Stats breakdown, the third tab, compares you with peers and with everyone in the U.S. Those shares are published counts. They are separate from the hazard model. Obesity by group is age-adjusted for adults 20 and older, NHANES August 2021–August 2023. There is no median BMI by race here, so your BMI is compared with the U.S. median for your sex. The cigarette bar is this page’s mix: 67 of 100 never smoked, 22 have quit, and 11 smoke now. Only current smoking is the published NHIS figure, 11.0% in 2023. The two quit choices share that 22. Diabetes peers are age-adjusted adults. The U.S. line is 12% of everyone, diagnosed or not. The American Indian and Alaska Native figure is diagnosed only. Heart attack and stroke shares are adults 20 and older, NHANES 2017–March 2020, from the American Heart Association 2025 statistics. That table has no obesity, heart-attack, or stroke figure for American Indian and Alaska Native adults. The activity bar is 26 of 100 with no leisure-time activity, 27 with some, and 47 who meet the guideline. A peer line for meeting the aerobic guideline is a different count from that inactive share. Diagnosed COPD is 3.8% of U.S. adults. By origin it is White 4.4%, Black 3.5%, Hispanic 2.0%, and Asian 1.0%. There is no separate COPD figure here for American Indian and Alaska Native adults. Kidney stage shares are stage 3 at 5.1%, stage 4 at 0.3%, and stage 5 at 0.2%. The chronic kidney disease bars are stages 1 through 4, which is a wider group than the stage 3 hazard.

Left out on purpose: blood pressure, cholesterol, cancer site and stage, medicines, family history, marital status, and place. Those move a real lifespan, and a single checkbox would fake a precision the studies do not support. State of residence stays out. In the 2022 NCHS state life tables, Hawaii is 80.0 years at birth and West Virginia is 72.2. Race and Hispanic origin stay in because you asked for the official tables. NCHS adjusts those tables for misclassification on the death certificate. The gaps mix smoking, work, place, and access to care.

Life expectancy at birth in these tables: 75.8 years for males and 81.1 for females in the U.S. total; Hispanic 78.5 and 84.0; American Indian and Alaska Native non-Hispanic 66.7 and 73.5; Asian non-Hispanic 83.2 and 87.1; Black non-Hispanic 70.3 and 77.6; White non-Hispanic 76.0 and 80.9.

You
Your median and average age at death, after height, weight, activity, smoking, illness, and personal income when you set it.
Peers
People your age, sex, and origin on the CDC table. Height, weight, activity, smoking, illness, and personal income are left out.
U.S. all people
Men’s median age at death to women’s, for everyone. It moves when your age changes, and it stays put for sex, origin, and health.
Line height
How many of 100 people in your group die at that age. A share of the life table, not a count of real people. The colored bands mark ages, not heights.
Median
The age half of that life table lives past.
Average
The mean age at death on that table. The headline runs from the lower of median and average to the higher, each rounded to the nearest year.
Years left
Remaining life expectancy from the age you entered, rounded to the nearest year.
Your years vs the median
How many years each entered factor adds to or removes from your peers’ CDC median. That median is the table for your age, sex, and origin. Personal income is included when you set it.
Total
Every counted factor at once. It is not the sum of the rows. Overlapping risks do not add.
BMI
Body mass index from your height and weight. The comparison median is U.S. men or U.S. women of all origins. There is no median BMI by race on this page.
of 100
The line height. 100 is the whole life-table group, not a count of real people.
Not specified
A field left blank. A blank is not treated as healthier than average.
Personal income
Your own earnings, not a household. At age 40, income can raise or lower life expectancy by about 5 years for men and about 3 years for women. Counted from age 40.
Another serious illness
Cancer, or an illness that does not have its own question. COPD and kidney disease are separate questions. This box does not move the median.
COPD
Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease. Usually emphysema or chronic bronchitis. A lasting illness that makes it hard to empty the lungs. It is not a cold or a short chest infection.
Emphysema
A kind of COPD. The air sacs in the lungs are damaged, so old air stays trapped.
Chronic bronchitis
A kind of COPD. The airways stay inflamed and make extra mucus for a long time. It is not one winter cold.
Long-term oxygen
Oxygen used at home because the lungs no longer keep the blood oxygen high enough. On this page it is grouped with a hospital stay for COPD.
Leisure-time activity
Movement you choose outside work, such as walking, sports, or cycling. A physical job is not counted. Not specified keeps the U.S. mix.
Activity guideline
About 150 minutes a week of moderate activity, such as a brisk walk, or 75 minutes of vigorous activity. Meeting it uses the published hazard for 1 to 2 times that minimum. More than that is not a separate choice.
Kidney disease stage 3
The kidneys filter less than they should. The filter number, eGFR, is about 30 to 59. This choice is not dialysis. Stage 4 is not its own choice. If you are not on dialysis, the page uses the stage 3 hazard. That hazard is the stage 3a figure with some albumin in the urine, so later stage 3 is understated.
Dialysis
A machine or fluid treatment that does the work of failed kidneys. On this page it is grouped with kidney failure.
eGFR
Estimated glomerular filtration rate. A blood-test estimate of how well the kidneys filter. About 90 or higher is the usual reference here. Stage 3 is about 30 to 59. The kidney-failure study used here is under 15.
Current age
The age you entered. The red dashed line marks it.
Origin
The NCHS group on the life table: Hispanic origin and race. It is a statistical death-rate group, not a biological ranking.
